# Research vision

<p class="page-lead">SMILE investigates a perception-informed engineering workflow: soundscape evidence helps define the problem, physical acoustics makes the target measurable, and acoustic metamaterials provide a design space for intervention.</p>

## The challenge

Acoustic engineering often begins with a level, spectrum, or regulatory threshold. Those quantities are essential, but they do not fully explain how an environment is perceived or whether a change is appropriate for its context.

Soundscape research starts from a complementary premise: an acoustic environment is perceived or experienced by people **in context**. Translating that evidence into an engineered structure is difficult. Perceptual outcomes are not material properties, and a reduction in one physical metric does not automatically imply a better experience.

SMILE addresses the translation problem by keeping three evidence layers explicit.

<div class="evidence-grid">
  <div class="evidence-item human">
    <strong>Human evidence</strong>
    Participant-reported outcomes such as pleasantness, appropriateness, perceived comfort, and annoyance.
  </div>
  <div class="evidence-item physical">
    <strong>Physical evidence</strong>
    Predicted and measured changes in transmission, reflection, absorption, spectrum, and spatial sound.
  </div>
  <div class="evidence-item practical">
    <strong>Practical evidence</strong>
    Robustness, space, mass, manufacturability, and sustainability within a defined application.
  </div>
</div>

## The working hypothesis

Context-specific soundscape evidence can guide the choice of acoustic targets. Physics-based models can then connect those targets to candidate structures. Machine-learning-assisted inverse design can search the resulting high-dimensional space more efficiently, while prototypes and participant studies test whether the predicted benefits are supported.

The project therefore asks not only **“How much sound is reduced?”**, but also:

- Which aspects of the sound field are relevant to the intended experience?
- Which physical changes can a feasible structure produce?
- How closely do simulations and measurements agree?
- Does the tested intervention change participant-reported outcomes in the intended direction?
- Over what operating and design range is the conclusion supported?

## Research principles

<div class="card-grid two">
  <div class="info-card">
    <span class="card-number">1</span>
    <h3>Context before optimisation</h3>
    <p>Targets are defined for a scenario and use, not treated as universal indicators of acoustic quality.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="info-card">
    <span class="card-number">2</span>
    <h3>Physics before claims</h3>
    <p>Candidate structures are assessed with measurable acoustic quantities, baselines, and uncertainty.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="info-card">
    <span class="card-number">3</span>
    <h3>People in the evidence loop</h3>
    <p>Perceptual benefit is evaluated in participant studies rather than inferred from physical performance alone.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="info-card">
    <span class="card-number">4</span>
    <h3>Bounded conclusions</h3>
    <p>Results are reported for the studied conditions and supported design range, with constraints made visible.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## What SMILE aims to produce

- A traceable workflow from contextual evidence to acoustic and engineering targets.
- Models and experimental methods for characterising candidate acoustic structures.
- Machine-learning-assisted tools for constrained, multi-objective inverse design.
- Prototypes and demonstrators assessed against physical and participant-based evidence.
- Publications and reusable outputs where ethics, licensing, and collaboration agreements permit.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>Important boundary.</strong> SMILE investigates candidate methods and interventions. References to adaptive, topological, or generative approaches describe parts of the available design space; they are not claims that every approach will become a validated project output.
</div>

## Scientific foundations

The soundscape framing follows the conceptual framework in [ISO 12913-1:2014](https://www.iso.org/standard/52161.html), which was reviewed and confirmed as current in 2025. The metamaterial research is situated within a rapidly developing field spanning fundamental wave control, noise mitigation, sensing, and device implementation.
